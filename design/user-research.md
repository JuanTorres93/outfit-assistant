## User Research Report — Outfit Application

## 1. Executive Summary

29-year-old female user working in professional environments including offices, meetings, conferences, and speaking engagements. Her style is defined as comfortable and elegant, and clothing serves a dual function for her: feeling good about herself and conveying an appropriate image for the context.

Her main problem is not a general inability to dress, but rather the friction of quickly finding valid alternatives within a large, low-visibility wardrobe, especially when an item is unavailable, uncomfortable that day, or doesn't fit the occasion.

In routine contexts, she usually solves the problem by resorting to known combinations. In important situations — conferences, parties, presentations, or speaking engagements — she dedicates more time and seeks to reduce the risk of making a mistake.

The main criterion for an application to provide value is very clear: it must enable her to reach a satisfactory outfit faster than doing it on her own.

## 2. User Profile

## Context

- 29 years old.
- Primarily works in professional environments.
- Frequent contexts:
    - Office.
    - Meetings.
    - Conferences.
    - Presentations.
    - Speaking engagements.
- She feels special pressure regarding her appearance when giving an important presentation or talk.
- In these contexts, she wants to attract attention intentionally, without being provocative and while maintaining a professional image.

## Habits

- Decides most outfits at the moment of dressing.
- Has little time available when getting ready for work.
- Can take about five minutes to decide on an outfit, an amount she already considers significant when in a hurry.
- Usually starts by selecting a specific item and looking for compatible pieces.
- If a piece stops working, she tries to keep the rest of the outfit and only replace that piece.
- If she can't find a suitable replacement, she redoes the entire ensemble.
- Uses known outfits as a quick solution.
- For important events, she tries on outfits beforehand and may save photos and notes with options she knows work.

## Level of Interest in Fashion

Moderate-high, but primarily outcome-oriented.

She is interested in:

- Looking good.
- Feeling attractive.
- Dressing appropriately.
- Experimenting with items inspired by external looks.
- Understanding which cuts might be flattering.

However, she does not seem interested in investing systematic effort in fashion for its own sake. Comfort and efficiency carry significant weight.

## Shopping Behavior

- Describes herself as quite impulsive.
- Shopping inspiration appears especially when:
    - Seeing looks on social media.
    - Seeing people on the street.
- She often doesn't check beforehand what she already has in her wardrobe.
- She does pay attention to the quality of materials.
- She usually wears what she buys if she remembers she has it.
- She has bought basic items she already owned because she didn't remember having something equivalent.
- When she buys something inspired by someone else, she usually reproduces a combination similar to the reference.

## Outfit Selection Behavior

The main criteria identified are:

1.  Occasion.
2.  Weather.
3.  Comfort.
4.  How she feels about her body that day.
5.  Social/professional appropriateness.
6.  Color.
7.  Silhouette and proportions.
8.  Avoiding certain repetitions.

In various contexts, she tries to keep parts of the outfit that already work and only replace the problematic item.

Example:
If she has chosen a t-shirt and the pants become uncomfortable, she tries to find similar pants before redoing the entire ensemble.

## Sources of Inspiration

- Social media.
- People on the street.
- External advice on which cuts flatter different body types.

Pinterest appears potentially as a secondary source of inspiration within the product.

## Current Tools

- Physical wardrobe.
- Weather application.
- Mobile notes.
- Personal outfit photos.
- Social media.
- References from other people.
- Observing how her colleagues dress.

## 3. Jobs to Be Done

## Functional Jobs

## JTBD 1 — Choose a suitable outfit quickly

When I have to get dressed and have little time, I want to quickly find a valid combination so I can leave without reviewing most of my wardrobe.

## JTBD 2 — Substitute an item without redoing the outfit

When a piece of the outfit is unavailable or doesn't work for me that day, I want to find a compatible alternative to keep the rest of the ensemble.

## JTBD 3 — Remember outfits that have already worked

When I have an important event similar to a previous one, I want to recover combinations with which I already felt good to reduce the risk of making a mistake.

## JTBD 4 — Adapt outfits to the context

When the weather, social situation, or level of formality changes, I want to adapt an outfit I like without necessarily starting from scratch.

## JTBD 5 — Know the contents of my wardrobe better

When I'm looking for what to wear or what to buy, I want to know what items I have to make better use of them and avoid forgetting them.

## JTBD 6 — Decide what clothes to keep

When I have too many clothes, I want to know what I actually use to decide what deserves to keep occupying space.

## Emotional Jobs

- Feel comfortable.
- Feel attractive.
- Feel confident in the outfit.
- Reduce doubt before leaving.
- Avoid feeling overly exposed.
- Feel that the outfit flatters the parts of her body she feels good about.

## Social Jobs

- Be perceived as professional.
- Not appear careless.
- Avoid a very obvious repetition being interpreted as poor hygiene.
- Not give the impression of having very few clothes.
- Not come across as too provocative when the context doesn't allow it.
- Fit in with the formality level of the environment.
- In certain presentations, stand out without losing professionalism.

## 5. Motivations

The motivations supported by the interview are:

## Save Time

This is the main success criterion for the product.

The app ceases to provide value if using it takes longer than selecting clothes manually.

## Feel Good About Her Appearance

She isn't only looking to follow fashion rules. The final validation is:

"How I look in the item."

## Feel Comfortable

Comfort appears repeatedly as a selection criterion and can change based on her physical state that day.

## Convey Professionalism

This is especially important in:

- Office.
- Meetings.
- Presentations.
- Conferences.
- Speaking engagements.

## Avoid Mistakes in Important Contexts

For this reason, she is willing to invest more time before a conference or a talk.

## Make Better Use of Her Clothes

She dislikes always wearing the same items and knows she probably has other forgotten alternatives.

## Have More Control Over Her Wardrobe

She is interested in knowing:

- What she uses.
- When she last used it.
- How many times she has used it.

Part of this interest is functional, and part is curiosity.

## 6. Frustrations

## Having to Review Too Many Clothes

She has several wardrobes and drawers, and some clothes remain stored in bags after seasonal changes.

This makes dressing up can become a physical search.

## Knowing She Has Options but Not Remembering Them

Revealing example:
When changing seasons, she found an item that had been put away and thought she could have used it when she was previously looking for something similar.

## Redoing an Outfit Because of One Piece

The natural strategy is to keep as much of the outfit as possible. Redoing it completely is perceived as a cost.

## Recommendations that Require Too Much Dialogue

She doesn't want to have to write explanations about why a recommendation doesn't work.

She prefers quick adjustments like:

- Tighter/Loosener.
- More/Less formal.
- More comfortable.
- More/Less warm.
- More/Less layers.
- Long/Short pants.

## Manually Maintaining Clothing Statuses

Recording that an item is:

- Being washed.
- Unavailable.

seems like too much effort.

She prefers to simply discard it if it appears in a recommendation.

## 7. Current Behavior

## "I need to get dressed" → "I decide what to wear"

## 1. Identifies the Context

## Considers:

- Type of event.
- Weather.
- Duration.
- People who will be there.
- Expected level of formality.

## 2. Reduces Options

She might start with a specific item or with known outfits.

## 3. Checks Compatibility

She mainly checks:

- Color.
- Proportions.
- Shape.

## Example:

If she wears wide pants, she prefers a somewhat tighter t-shirt.

## 4. Checks Comfort and Body

She may know beforehand which cuts are likely to be comfortable, but often needs to try them on.

The body's state that day can change the choice of:

- Fitted vs. loose.
- With/without neckline.
- Long/Short pants.

## 5. Checks Appropriateness

At work, she evaluates if the outfit:

- Seems professional enough.
- Is too casual.
- Might seem too provocative.

To determine this, she observes how her professional environment dresses.

## 6. Checks for Repetition

Especially important for tops.

She avoids, when possible, repeating a t-shirt or shirt two days in a row.

## 7. Replaces Problematic Items

If an item fails:

1.  Looks for a similar alternative.
2.  Tries to keep the other clothes.
3.  If she doesn't find a compatible replacement, she redoes the outfit.

## 8. Resorts to Safe Outfits

When she needs speed or security, she uses known combinations.

## 9. For Important Events: Validates in Advance

## She may:

- Try on outfits.
- Take photos of herself.
- Create a note with alternatives.
- Recover outfits from previous events.

## 8. Product Needs

## Explicit Needs

The user expresses interest in:

- Proposing alternatives quickly.
- Organizing her wardrobe better.
- Knowing what she uses and what she doesn't.
- Recovering outfits that already work.
- Adapting them to the weather.
- Filtering by comfort and body.
- Incorporating items easily.
- Correcting recommendations through quick options.

## Implicit Needs

## "Substitute Without Recomposing"

The product should understand the outfit as a partially reusable structure.

It's not always necessary to generate a new ensemble.

## External Wardrobe Memory

The user needs to offload some of the memory required to know what she has.

## Learning Through Minimum Feedback

There is potential to learn from:

- Accepted items.
- Rejected items.
- Modified parameters.
- Repeated outfits.
- Contexts.

without forcing her to explain every decision.

## Social Context as a Variable

Appropriateness doesn't only depend on labels like "party" or "work."

It also depends on:

- Level of familiarity with people.
- Professional exposure.
- Desire to stand out or blend in.

## Immediate Value with Incomplete Wardrobe

The product must be able to work even if only part of the wardrobe has been registered.

## Unmet Needs

- Having a complete and accessible view of the wardrobe.
- Quickly finding equivalent substitutes.
- Remembering successful outfits with their context.
- Knowing which items are actually used.
- Adapting existing outfits to small changes in conditions.

## 10. Features That Should NOT Be Built Yet

## 1. Manual Tracking of Items Being Washed/Unavailable

The update frequency would be too high.

The user prefers:

discarding a specific recommendation

rather than keeping that data updated.

## 2. Forcing Upload of Body Photos

The user would prefer the system to work with:

- Measurements.
- Preferences.
- Body proportions.

There is no evidence that a body photo provides enough additional value.

## 3. Mandatory Textual Feedback

Asking:

"Explain why you don't like this proposal"

would create friction.

It should be replaced by structured controls.

## 4. Generating Many Alternatives by Default

The user prefers:

1.  One main proposal.
2.  Alternatives if the main one fails.

## 5. Negative Messages About the Body

Avoid expressions like:

"This item doesn't flatter you."

Prefer positive, goal-oriented recommendations.

## 11. Unforeseen Opportunities

## Opportunity 1 — Wardrobe Lifecycle Management

## Problem

She struggles to distinguish between:

- "I don't use it now."
- "I probably shouldn't keep it anymore."

## Evidence

She keeps clothes she currently doesn't use because they might fit her again if she loses or gains weight.

## Importance

The user's problem doesn't end with "what to wear"; it also includes what deserves to remain part of my wardrobe.

## Solution to Explore

- Last date used.
- Number of uses.
- Season history.
- Classification as "temporarily out of rotation."
- Review suggestions, not automatic deletion.

## Opportunity 2 — Outfit as a System of Replaceable Components

## Problem

When one piece fails, the user tries to keep the others.

## Evidence

This behavior appeared repeatedly regarding work, body comfort, and social events.

## Importance

This is likely a more frequent use case than "create a completely new outfit for me."

## Solution

## Action:

"Change this item"

with parameters like:

- More comfortable.
- Looser.
- More formal.
- Warmer.
- Similar color.
- Same visual function.

## Opportunity 3 — Learn Which Items are Visually "Repeatable"

## Problem

Not all items have the same social repetition cost.

## Evidence

The user distinguishes:

## Highly Visible

- Dresses.
- Shirts.
- T-shirts.

## Repeatable

- Basic pants.
- Jackets.
- Shoes.

## Solution

Model the "prominence level" of each item to plan multiple days.

## Opportunity 4 — Planner for Trips/Conferences

## Problem

A multi-day event requires optimization, not simply several independent outfits.

## Evidence

She deliberately reuses:

- Basic pants.
- Jackets.
- Shoes.

and seeks variety in more visible items.

## Solution

Generate multiple outfits simultaneously, minimizing luggage and perceptible repetition.

## Opportunity 5 — Interpersonal Context

## Problem

The same item can feel appropriate or too provocative depending on who will be present.

## Evidence

At a birthday party where she only knew the guest of honor, she changed her top because she felt too accentuated.

## Solution

Explore context levels such as:

- Trusted environment.
- Acquaintances.
- Professional.
- High exposure.

Instead of relying solely on event categories.

## 13. Target User Profile

## What type of person seems like the best target user?

A person who:

- Has a relatively large wardrobe.
- Doesn't remember all their items.
- Has multiple possible combinations.
- Has contexts with different dress codes.
- Needs to decide on clothes frequently.
- Values feeling good and appropriate.
- Has known combinations but wants more alternatives.
- Is willing to digitize their wardrobe progressively.
- Accepts automatic recommendations as long as they reduce time.

The problem appears several times a week, although with varying intensity.

The opportunity is especially clear for people who combine:

large wardrobe + little time + multiple contexts + moderate/high concern about image.

## Who does NOT seem like a good target user?

## People who:

- Have a small, fully visible wardrobe.
- Always wear practically the same clothes and are satisfied.
- Don't consider how they combine clothes important.
- Have a lot of time and enjoy the manual process of experimenting each morning.
- Are not willing to register any part of their wardrobe.
- Expect the app to automatically know all their items without any input.
- Barely experience changes in context or dress code.

## 14. Recommendations for the Development Team

## The 5 Things We Must Understand About the User

1.  The goal is not to generate creativity; it's to reduce time and friction.
2.  An outfit that already works has a lot of value and doesn't need reinventing.
3.  Comfort can vary from one day to another even if the item is the same.
4.  Appropriateness depends as much on the occasion as on the people and the professional environment.
5.  Wardrobe visibility is almost as important as the recommendation algorithm.

## The 5 Product Priorities

## 1. Low-Friction Digital Wardrobe

High via:

- URL.
- Repository.
- Receipt/reference.
- Manual photo.

Always with confirmation before automatic uploads.

## 2. Intelligent Single-Item Substitution

Probably one of the most important use cases.

## 3. Quick Main Recommendation + Structured Refinement

Avoid forcing navigation through many alternatives.

## 4. Memory of Validated Outfits

Save:

- Context.
- Weather.
- Occasion.
- Feelings/preferences.

and allow repetition.

## 5. Contextual Adaptation

Especially:

- Weather.
- Formality.
- Comfort.
- Fit level.
- Exposure.
- Event duration.

## The 5 Things We Should NOT Assume

1.  Do not assume the user constantly wants new outfits.
2.  Do not assume maintaining the availability of each item is worth the effort.
3.  Do not assume a theoretical recommendation for her body type will be accepted.
4.  Do not assume repeating clothes is always a major problem.
5.  Do not assume more alternatives produce more value.

## The 5 Questions We Still Need to Answer

## 1. What is the maximum acceptable interaction time?

We know that five failed recommendations might be too many, but we need to measure the total tolerable time.

## 2. How much wardrobe needs to be digitized for recommendations to start being good?

The user will likely upload it progressively.

## 3. How to register real item usage with almost zero effort?

Necessary for statistics and wardrobe decluttering.

## 4. What attributes does the model really need for each item?

There is evidence for:

- Color.
- Cut.
- Fit.
- Length.
- Formality.
- Thickness.
- Visual prominence.

But we still need to validate which are essential.

## 5. Which learning mechanism builds trust fastest?

We need to compare:

- Explicit feedback.
- Acceptance/Rejection.
- Outfit history.
- Manual adjustments.
- Voluntary repetition.

## Product Conclusion

The most solid opportunity does not seem to be simply:

## "An AI that creates outfits."

The proposal with the most support from this interview would be something closer to:

"A digital wardrobe that remembers what you have, recovers combinations that already work, and quickly finds substitutions or adaptations when an item, your comfort, the weather, or the context changes."

Creative generation, Pinterest, and purchase recommendations can expand the product later, but they do not seem to be the core of the initial value.

The biggest product risk is building a technically sophisticated tool that requires more interaction than opening the wardrobe and putting on a known ensemble.

The central metric should reflect precisely the opposite:

*How often does the app take the user from "I need to get dressed" to "this outfit works for me" faster than their current process?*
